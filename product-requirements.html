01

Product concept & positioning

Swaram is not a complaint form. A form produces a private ticket that disappears into an inbox. Swaram produces a public, shared civic object that many people can point at, argue about, follow, and hold someone accountable for.

Surface 01

Citizen App

Behaves like a social platform. Discover → Report → Discuss → Support → Follow. Mobile-first, thumb-driven, works on a cheap Android over 3G.

Surface 02

Government Portal

Behaves like Linear/Jira. Receive → Assign → Work → Resolve → Verify. Dense, keyboard-driven, desktop-first but usable on a field phone.

Surface 03

Admin Dashboard

For the platform operator, not for line officers. Ecosystem health, department SLAs, geographic hotspots, AI quality metrics.

What we are building

  • One public object per real-world problem. Not one ticket per angry citizen.
  • Reporting that requires zero bureaucratic knowledge. Speak, photograph, submit. The AI decides department, ward, category and priority.
  • Visible pressure. Report counts, follower counts and elapsed time are public. Silence becomes expensive.
  • An operational tool officers actually prefer. If the portal is worse than WhatsApp, they will use WhatsApp.
  • An auditable AI trail. Every automated decision is logged with input, output and confidence, and is overridable by a human.

What we are explicitly not building

  • Not a general social network. No follows-between-people, no DMs, no vanity profiles. You follow issues, not humans.
  • Not an anonymous rage board. Reports are attributable to a verified phone number, even where the display is pseudonymous.
  • Not a replacement for emergency services. Fire, medical and crime route to 112 with a hard interstitial.
  • Not an AI that closes tickets by itself. AI proposes; a human disposes on every state change that affects a citizen.
The core bet Most civic apps fail because the citizen's effort is high and the visible return is zero. Swaram inverts both: reporting takes under 30 seconds, and the reporter immediately joins a visible crowd of 27 other people with the same problem. Clustering is not a backend optimisation — it is the product.
02

Problem, users & principles

The citizen's problem

A pothole has been there for three weeks. You don't know which department owns it, which ward you're in, or whether anyone else has complained. If you do complain, you get a reference number and silence. You cannot tell whether you are the first person or the fiftieth.

The officer's problem

Fifty complaints arrive for one pothole across phone, WhatsApp, email and a portal. Half are mis-routed to your department. There is no single record of the problem, so "is it fixed?" cannot be answered without a site visit and a phone call.

Personas

P-01

Rahul — The Reporter

28, commutes daily, mid-range Android, patchy data. Will give the app 30 seconds while standing at the spot. Motivated by "did anything happen?"

Needs: instant capture, zero forms, proof of progress.

P-02

Priya — The Watcher

41, RWA member. Rarely reports, constantly monitors. Wants to know what is happening in a 2 km radius and to amplify what matters.

Needs: feed, filters, follow, share.

P-03

Officer Kiran — The Resolver

Ward-level engineer, Roads. 30–60 live items. Desktop in the office, phone in the field. Judged on closure time and re-open rate.

Needs: a sane queue, bulk actions, one-tap field updates with photo.

P-04

Supervisor Meera — The Accountable

Zone-level. Cares about overdue counts, department SLA breaches and escalations, not individual tickets.

Needs: dashboards, reassignment, escalation, exports.

Product principles

01 — The crowd is the feature

Every surface should make it obvious how many people share a problem. A count of 27 is more persuasive than any wording.

02 — Never ask what we can infer

Department, ward, category, severity and duplicates are inferred. The citizen answers one question: what happened?

03 — Confidence must be visible

Where AI decided something, say so, show the confidence, and let a human change it in one tap.

04 — Status is a promise

Every status change fires a notification to reporters and followers. No silent transitions, ever.

05 — Thumb-first, then keyboard

Citizen surfaces are designed for one hand at arm's length. Government surfaces are designed for a keyboard and a large screen.

06 — Slow networks are the default

Optimistic UI, offline draft queue, aggressive image compression on-device. Assume 3G and 2 GB RAM.

Why the split matters

  • An Issue is the real-world problem. It has a status, an owner and an SLA. There is exactly one per physical problem.
  • A Report is one person's experience of it. It has an author, a photo, a timestamp and a precise location. There are many per issue.
  • Government works the Issue. Officers never see 27 tickets; they see one ticket with 27 pieces of evidence.
  • Citizens contribute Reports. "I'm facing this too" creates a Report attached to an existing Issue, not a new Issue.
  • Metrics are honest. "27 people affected" comes from report count; "how many problems exist" comes from issue count. Conflating them destroys both numbers.
Hard rule Anyone can create an issue, always. A submission is never blocked, diverted or converted at submit time because "a similar issue exists". Every report creates its own Issue immediately. Clustering happens afterwards, asynchronously, and is decided by the agent — not by a radius rule at the point of capture.

Community verification — resolution is not a government declaration

A problem is not resolved because an authority says it is. After a Resolution Submission, the people who actually reported the issue judge it — and the outcome is a distribution, not a boolean.

What citizens are asked

  • Completely fixed
  • Partially fixed — with a prompt for what remains
  • Still exists — with an option to add fresh photo evidence
  • New problem created — the repair caused something else

Only reporters and followers of that issue are asked, and only those whose location makes them plausible witnesses. Everyone can see the result.

The satisfaction score

The public outcome is the breakdown itself, not a verdict:

72%  Completely fixed
19%  Partially fixed
 7%  Still exists
 2%  New problem created

→ RESOLVED · satisfaction 72%
  • Quorum: whichever comes first — responses from 20% of reporters, at least 3 people, or a 7-day window.
  • Below ~50% "completely fixed" the issue does not close; it returns to active with pressure raised and the officer notified.
  • Low turnout closes the issue at the window's end, but records "insufficient verification" rather than manufacturing consent.
  • Satisfaction, not closure count, is the department metric. Closing fast with 30% satisfaction must look worse than closing slowly at 90%.
  • Tier 2 and 3 issues (§12) can still be community-verified — a citizen confirming a pothole was quietly filled is valuable even when no officer ever logged in.

Location is always required — but precision and publicity are not the same thing

Location is not decoration; it is what determines who owns the issue. Without it there is no jurisdiction, so no department, no officer, no SLA and no accountability — the issue has nowhere to go. Even a harassment complaint needs a location, because it decides which police station is answerable. So location stays mandatory. What varies is how precise it is, and who is allowed to see it.

Location precision — three tiers

  • Point — a pothole, a broken streetlight, a dumped pile. An exact pin. The default.
  • Segment or area — a water outage on a street, a blocked drain along a stretch, a power cut across a block. The issue owns a line or polygon, not a dot, and the feed shows "along 3rd Cross" rather than a misleading single pin.
  • Jurisdiction-level — a ward-wide service failure or an administrative grievance where no specific spot exists. Routes on ward alone. Rare, but it must not be forced into a fake pin.

Precision is inferred by the classifier from the category and description, and is adjustable by the officer. It also feeds clustering: an area-tier issue uses a far wider candidate net than a point-tier one.

Location visibility — independent of precision

  • Exact — shown publicly on the map. Correct for infrastructure.
  • Approximate — the public sees a ~200 m blurred area while the officer sees the true point. Used automatically when a report is at or near the reporter's home, so reporting a problem outside your gate does not publish your address.
  • Private — the location is never public at any resolution. Only the owning department sees it.
What your harassment example actually exposes You were right that location is needed there — but that case breaks something bigger than location. A woman reporting harassment cannot have her report sitting on a public civic feed with a photo, a map pin, a comment thread and a "27 people also facing this" counter. The problem is not the coordinates; it is that the feed is public by default and some issues must never be public at all. See the visibility classes below.

  • The classifier proposes the class; it fails safe. Categories touching personal safety, minors or named individuals default to CONFIDENTIAL, and a human must actively downgrade. Wrongly private is a recoverable mistake; wrongly public is not.
  • The reporter can raise the class at any time, on any issue, in one tap — but only a supervisor can lower it, and lowering is logged.
  • Confidential issues still get an SLA, an owner and a full timeline. They lose the audience, not the accountability.
  • Emergency interception still applies first. An in-progress assault is a 112 call, not a ticket — the interstitial in §08 fires before any of this.
  • Whether Swaram should accept this category at all is an open question — see §22. Building a half-serious channel for a serious harm is worse than routing it to the proper authority and saying so.

06

Feed, filters & ranking

Feed card — required content

  • Status + priority pills — always first, always visible without expanding.
  • Title — AI-generated, max 2 lines, human-editable by the officer.
  • Distance from user — "500 m away", not coordinates. The single strongest relevance cue.
  • Department + public ID — establishes accountability and shareability.
  • Crowd count — "27 people reported this" is the emotional payload of the card.
  • Age / last activity — "2d old", "officer updated 4h ago".
  • Lead media — first photo, lazy-loaded, blur-up placeholder, 16:9 crop.
  • Three actions — Support (♥ affected), Comment, Follow. Nothing else.
Anti-requirement No reporter avatars or names on the feed card. The subject is the problem, not the person. Attribution lives on the issue page, where the reports are listed.

Three numbers that must never collapse into one

The most common failure in a civic platform is letting popularity become importance. A viral complaint about a gated community's driveway must not outrank a sewage overflow next to a school that nobody has tweeted about. So we keep three separate numbers, computed differently, shown differently, and used for different decisions.

Severity — objective

How dangerous or damaging the problem is, judged from the report content by the intake agent. A live electrical hazard is CRITICAL with one report and zero supporters.

Drives: priority, SLA, emergency interception. Never influenced by report count.

Civic pressure — social

How strongly the community is demanding action: reports, supporters, followers, discussion activity, and how long it has gone unresolved. Shown as a 0–100 score.

Drives: feed ranking, escalation candidacy, the weekly digest. Never drives SLA.

People affected — estimated

An estimate, not a count — inferred from the affected area, locality context, nearby schools or facilities, and refined as reports arrive. "~2,000 people" for a contaminated supply across three complexes.

Drives: priority weighting and scale escalation. Always displayed as an estimate with a ~.

  • Pressure rises when a resolution is rejected. If citizens reject the government's submitted resolution, or fresh reports arrive after closure, pressure increases rather than resetting — the issue returns to active with more force than before. This is the accountability loop's teeth.
  • Pressure decays when an authority is genuinely engaging: acknowledgement, official replies and progress evidence all dampen it. It measures unmet demand, not hostility.
  • Never show pressure to the officer as a leaderboard. On the government surface it is context, not a scoreboard — officers who feel publicly ranked start rejecting issues instead of fixing them.
  • Estimated people affected is capped in its influence on priority, and the estimate itself is officer-correctable. An AI estimate of "~50,000 affected" must not be able to hijack a department's whole queue.

Ranking

The feed is not chronological. An issue affecting 500 people must outrank a fresh minor complaint. For V1 a transparent scoring formula beats a learned model — it is explainable, debuggable and demo-able.

score =
    w1 · proximity      (1 / (1 + km))
  + w2 · severity       (LOW…CRITICAL → 0.2…1.0)
  + w3 · report_count   (log scale, capped)
  + w4 · recent_activity(decay over 72 h)
  + w5 · people_affected(est. from location type)
  − w6 · staleness      (resolved & old sinks)

defaults: w1 .30  w2 .20  w3 .20
          w4 .15  w5 .10  w6 .05
  • Log-scale the report count so the 200th report doesn't permanently pin one issue to the top.
  • Resolved issues stay in the feed for 48 h with before/after media. Visible wins are what keep people reporting.
  • Guaranteed diversity: no more than 3 consecutive cards from the same department.
  • Fresh-report boost: an issue with a report in the last hour gets a temporary multiplier so live problems surface.
  • Weights are server-side config, tunable without a client release, and logged per feed request for debugging.

Crowd block

  • Reported → Assigned → In progress → Resolved

08

Report flow

Target: under 30 seconds from tapping ＋ to a confirmed submission, standing at the location, one-handed.

The flow

  1. Tap ＋ from anywhere. Camera and location permissions are requested here, in context, with a plain-language reason — never on app launch.
  2. Capture. One screen, four equal inputs: hold-to-speak voice, photo, video, text. Any one is sufficient to submit.
  3. Location. Auto-filled from GPS with a reverse-geocoded address. One tap to adjust the pin. Manual entry if GPS is denied or wrong.
  4. AI preview. While the user is still on the screen, show the inferred department, category and priority, plus a passive note if similar reports exist nearby. This is information, never a gate — it cannot stop or redirect the submission.
  5. Submit. Optimistic: the UI confirms immediately, upload continues in the background, and failures retry from a local queue. The issue is always created.
  6. Confirmation. Screen 05 — the new issue's public ID, what the AI decided and why, and a note that clustering is still being checked.
  7. Later — the merge notification. If the clustering agent merges the issue, everyone involved gets "Your report was combined with 26 others — CIV-10234". This is where the crowd moment now lands.

Requirements

  • Voice is a first-class input. Kannada, Hindi, English, Telugu at minimum. Transcription happens server-side; the raw audio is retained as evidence.
  • No category picker. Ever. If the AI is unsure it asks a single disambiguating question ("Is this about the road surface or the drain?"), never a 9-item dropdown.
  • Client-side image compression to ≤ 1600 px long edge, ~200 KB target, before upload. EXIF GPS is read then stripped from the stored copy.
  • Offline capture. Reports queue locally with their media and submit when connectivity returns; the user sees "Queued — will send when you're online".
  • Emergency interception. If the classifier detects fire, injury, crime or gas leak, an interstitial appears with a one-tap call to 112 before submission continues.
  • Anonymous-to-public toggle. The user chooses whether their display name appears on the public report. Identity is always known to the platform for accountability.
  • Duplicate handling is never a wall. If a match is found, the copy is "join 26 others" — a benefit, not a rejection.
09

My Issues, alerts, profile & about

My Issues — Created

Issues where the user authored a report. Sorted by last activity. Each row: ID, title, status pill, report count, last officer update, SLA remaining.

Key action: on a RESOLVED issue within the 14-day window, a "Still a problem? Reopen" link.

My Issues — Following

Issues the user follows without reporting. Same row anatomy, plus an unfollow swipe action. Empty state points to the feed: "Follow issues to track them here."

Alerts

Grouped by day. Types: status change, official reply, comment reply, issue resolved, reopen window closing, new high-priority issue near you.

Rule: tapping any alert deep-links to the exact section of the issue page that changed.

Profile & settings

Name, phone (verified), home ward, language, notification preferences per type and channel, privacy controls, delete account and export my data.

Civic reputation — reward outcomes, never volume

Badges are in scope, with one non-negotiable constraint: nothing is ever awarded for submitting reports. Every badge must be earned on the far side of an outcome the user did not fully control — a verified resolution, a merge that consolidated real duplicates, an issue the community rallied behind. Reward the volume of complaints and you will get volume, and an officer's day pays for it.

  • Outcome-gated: Eyes Open (first verified issue) · Pathfinder (routed and acknowledged) · Community Builder (reports consolidated into a master issue) · Voice of the People (issue reached significant support) · Problem Solver (contributed to a verified resolution) · Local Guardian (sustained high-impact contribution in a district).
  • No citizen leaderboards. Badges are personal and appear on your own profile. Ranking citizens against each other turns civic reporting into competition, and competition into noise.
  • Leaderboards are for governments, not people. Ranking districts and states by resolution performance is the opposite mechanism — it points accountability upward, where it belongs.
  • Badges are invisible on the feed and on issue pages. They never decorate a report or lend it apparent weight.

About section

  • What is Swaram — one paragraph, no jargon.
  • How it works — Report → AI understands → Government acts → Verified → Resolved, as a five-step visual.
  • How the AI works — plainly: what it decides, what confidence means, that a human can override it, and how to flag a wrong decision.
  • Privacy — what location data is stored, what is public, what is never shared.
  • Community guidelines — what belongs here, what does not, how flags work.
  • Contact, terms, open data — including a public statistics page per ward.

The queue

  • Views are saved filters over a single table: My work, Unassigned, Overdue, Awaiting verification, Team, All. Officers can save their own.
  • Default sort: SLA remaining ascending — what breaches first is on top. Not newest-first.
  • Row anatomy: ID · title · status · priority · report count · assignee avatar · SLA countdown · last activity.
  • Bulk actions: multi-select for assign, priority, status, and merge duplicates.
  • Overdue rows are unmissable — negative SLA in critical red with a left edge marker.
  • Command palette (⌘K): jump to ticket by ID, change status, assign, search.

The ticket

  • Properties rail: status, priority, department, jurisdiction, assignee, SLA, report count, follower count, coordinates with a map link.
  • Tab: Reports — all 27 citizen reports with photo, text/voice, exact location and time. The evidence base.
  • Tab: Discussion — a single thread with a visibility toggle per message: Public (citizens see it) or Internal (department only). This toggle must be impossible to get wrong.
  • Tab: Evidence — initial, progress and resolution media, each geotagged and timestamped.
  • Tab: Activity — the immutable timeline: who did what, when, and which AI agent decided what with what confidence.
  • There is no "Mark Resolved" button. The authority files a Resolution Submission: what was done or will be done, plus evidence — before/after photos, work orders, completion reports, documents, GPS-stamped site imagery. A one-click close is what makes existing grievance portals worthless.
  • The authority does not get the last word. Submitting a resolution moves the issue to community verification (§03), not to resolved.

Officers can create issues too

Officers report what they observe in the field, internal infrastructure faults, and problems belonging to another department. The same AI pipeline runs — classification, jurisdiction, routing, priority — so a cross-department hand-off is one action, not an email.

Extra fields officers get: pre-set priority, internal-only flag, and direct assignment on creation.

Field mode — the mobile officer

The portal on a phone is not a shrunken table. It is a task list of today's assigned issues sorted by distance from the officer, with one-tap actions: acknowledge, start work, upload progress photo, mark resolved with photo.

Requirement: resolution photos capture GPS and time, and the AI verifier compares them against the original report location and imagery.

Many people, one accountable owner

A real pothole repair involves a ward engineer, a road contractor, a field crew, a supervisor watching the SLA, and sometimes a corporator or minister whose constituency it is. A single assigned_officer field cannot express that — but neither can a free-for-all where five people are "on it" and nobody is answerable.

The rule that keeps this from becoming a group chat An issue has exactly one Owner at all times. Ownership is transferable but never shared, never null, and never plural. Everyone else is a participant with a defined role. When an SLA breaches, exactly one name is attached to it — otherwise accountability evaporates the moment it matters.

Requirements

  • Contractors get scoped accounts — they see only the issues they are attached to, never the queue, never the citizen's identity. A contractor login must not become a back door into the whole ward's data.
  • A contractor cannot close an issue. They submit resolution evidence; the Owner accepts or rejects it. The party paid to do the work never certifies its own completion.
  • Representatives are read-and-comment only. A minister on the thread must not be able to change priority or reassign — political pressure should be visible in the record, not exercised through the tool. Their comments are marked Representative, distinct from Official.
  • Adding a participant is logged with who added them and why, and appears in the activity timeline.
  • Ownership transfer is explicit — the new owner must acknowledge, and the SLA clock does not reset on an intra-department handover (only on a genuine cross-department re-route).
  • The citizen sees a roster, not a list of strangers: "Roads Dept · Officer Kiran · with ABC Infra (contractor)". Enough to know who is accountable, not enough to harass anyone.
  • Notifications respect roles — the contractor is not paged about every citizen comment; the representative is not paged about SLA internals.

The accumulated data is not just an internal dashboard. Turned outward, it is the product's civic contribution and its distribution engine.

  • Interactive civic map — public, zoomable India → state → district → local. Editorial responsibility An auto-generated newspaper naming departments and districts is a publication. Accountability rules

  • Department performance is publishable. A public statistics page per ward is a first-class feature, not an afterthought — resolution rates are the product's civic output.
  • No silent metric edits. Admin cannot delete issues to improve numbers; only merge, reject with reason, or close. All are logged with the actor.
  • AI metrics are measured against human overrides, not self-reported confidence. Confidence is a signal, accuracy is the truth.

The gap this exposes Jurisdiction coverage is genuinely national. Officer coverage is roughly 129 named contacts for the entire country. Everything else in this document — the queue, the assignment, the SLA, the officer replies — assumes an onboarded officer at the other end. For most locations, on day one, there is no such person in the system. A product that only works where an officer has signed up is a product that works in one ward.

Three routing tiers — the product must work in all three

Tier 1 · Onboarded

A real officer account exists

The full product as specified: assignment, SLA clock, official replies, resolution evidence, community verification. This is the target state, and initially it will be a handful of wards.

Tier 2 · Contactable

We have a grievance contact, not an account

The issue is real and routed, but no one is logged in. Swaram emails a formatted grievance to the registry contact, tracks delivery, and shows the citizen "Sent to [authority] · awaiting response". No fake SLA countdown, no fake assignee.

Tier 3 · Unmapped

Jurisdiction known, no contact at all

The issue is published, clustered and counted. Its function is public pressure and evidence accumulation. Status reads "Published · no authority contact yet" — honestly, not as a pending ticket.

  • Never fabricate an assignee or an SLA. A countdown against a department that has never heard of Swaram is a lie to the citizen and the fastest way to lose them. Tier 2 and 3 issues show what actually happened: sent, or published and waiting.
  • Coverage is a first-class product surface. The citizen is told, at report time, what will realistically happen with their report in their area. "We'll notify the Ward 42 Roads officer" and "we'll email the state grievance cell" are different promises and must read differently.
  • Tier 3 is not failure — it is the wedge. An unmapped area accumulating 200 clustered, evidenced, publicly-visible issues is precisely the argument for onboarding that body. Coverage grows because the pressure exists, not before it.
  • Every tier still gets clustering, pressure and verification. The civic loop does not depend on government participation; only resolution does.
  • The utility-board and city-portal datasets must be verified before they route anything. Sending a citizen's report to a wrong or dead address is worse than telling them we have no contact.

Scale escalation — a separate ladder from SLA escalation

§10's escalation is temporal: an SLA breached, so a supervisor is notified. This ladder is about scale: an issue outgrowing the authority it was routed to. A pothole stays local forever. A contaminated water supply across three apartment complexes does not.

   Local  →  Ward  →  City  →  District  →  State  →  National

promotion triggers (any two, plus officer or supervisor confirmation):

  • Promotion adds an authority, it does not remove one. The ward body stays on the issue as a participant when it escalates to district — otherwise escalation becomes a way to shed responsibility.

  • Escalation is visible to citizens on the issue page, as a rung on the ladder — it is one of the most motivating things the product can show.
  • Non-geographic large issues — exam irregularities, policy failures — enter at the tier that matches their scope rather than being forced through a local body first.

Intake is deliberately one agent, not four. Classifying a pothole photo, reading its severity and titling it are a single reasoning pass over a single input — splitting them buys nothing and costs three round trips.

Transparency requirements

  • Every AI decision is logged with input, output, confidence, model and latency, and is visible in the ticket's AI trace.
  • Confidence is shown, not hidden — "Routed to Roads · 94%" on the officer's ticket, and in plain language on the citizen's confirmation.
  • Below threshold → human queue. Low confidence never fails silently; it lands in an "AI unsure" triage view for a supervisor.
  • One-tap override everywhere. Officers change department, category or priority in one action; every override is recorded as training signal.
  • The AI never closes an issue. The verifier produces a recommendation; a human confirms. Auto-closing is the fastest way to lose citizen trust permanently.
  • Citizens can dispute a decision — "This isn't a Roads problem" — which flags the issue for re-routing.

Clustering — the hardest decision in the system

Anyone can create an issue. Two people standing at the same pothole will create two issues, and that is correct behaviour — the system's job is to reconcile them afterwards, not to interrogate the second person at submit time.

Why radius alone cannot do this A pothole and the pothole 40 m down the same road are two different problems. A water main break and a report 300 m away on the same street are one problem. No single distance threshold is correct for both, and no fixed per-category radius survives contact with real geography — a "30 m pothole rule" merges two distinct craters on a wide junction and splits one long trench. Distance is a candidate filter, never the decision.

The three signals the agent weighs together

  • Location — proximity, but also whether the two points sit on the same road segment, the same side of it, and inside the same ward. 80 m along one road is closer, civically, than 20 m across a divider.
  • Image — visual similarity of the reported media: is this the same crater, the same pile, the same pole? This is what separates two potholes from one, and it is the signal a radius rule has no access to.
  • Description — semantic similarity of text and voice transcripts, including landmark mentions ("near the signal", "opposite the school") that are often more precise than the GPS fix itself.

Any one signal alone is unreliable. Location alone over-merges. Description alone merges every pothole in the city. Images alone fail at night and in the rain. The merge decision requires agreement across at least two, with location as a mandatory gate.

Merge policy

ConfidenceWhat happens
≥ 0.90Agent merges automatically. Both sets of reports move to the surviving issue; everyone involved is notified.
0.75 – 0.90Queued as a suggested merge in the officer's queue with the two photos side by side. One tap to confirm or dismiss.
< 0.75Nothing happens. The issues stay separate. Silence is the safe default.
  • The older issue survives a merge, keeping its public ID, its history and its SLA clock. The younger one redirects to it permanently — shared links never break.
  • Merges are reversible. "Split issue" is a first-class officer action, and the reports remember which issue they originally belonged to.
  • Re-clustering runs continuously, not just at creation. An issue that gets its third report with a clearer photo may become mergeable an hour later.
  • Never merge across departments or across a resolved/open boundary without officer confirmation.
  • Every merge is logged in agent_runs with all three signal scores, so a bad merge can be explained and the threshold tuned.

Failure modes to design for

False merge

Two distinct potholes 40 m apart merged into one. The officer fixes one and closes both, and 12 people are told their problem is solved when it isn't. Mitigation: image similarity is mandatory for auto-merge, the officer "split issue" action is first-class, and any citizen on a merged issue can dispute it in one tap. This is the most damaging error the system can make — the thresholds should be tuned to under-merge.

Mis-routing

A drainage issue sent to Roads. Mitigation: one-tap "not my department" with a suggested destination; re-routing resets the SLA clock and is logged against routing accuracy.

Bad transcription

Dialect or noise produces nonsense. Mitigation: show the transcript back to the user before submit with an edit affordance, and always keep the original audio attached as evidence.

Semantic colour

Status colour is a semantic token, never a decorative choice. The same colour means the same thing on every surface, including the map and the charts.

Identity & verification — how we know who someone is

Two different questions get conflated constantly, and answering the wrong one creates a security hole: "is this a real person?" and "does this person hold this office?" The first is identity. The second is authorisation, and it can only come from the employer.

Aadhaar cannot tell us someone is a government official Aadhaar holds name, date of birth, gender, address and biometrics. It carries no employment, department or designation data, and no API returns any. Aadhaar eKYC can confirm that Kiran is really Kiran; it can never confirm that Kiran works for the Roads Department in Ward 42. Any design that infers officer status from an Aadhaar-linked phone number is inferring a fact that does not exist in the source.

Citizens — phone is sufficient

  • Phone OTP is the anchor. Indian SIMs are already KYC'd at issuance, so a verified number is a traceable real identity. This gives us one-account-per-person and abuse traceability without touching Aadhaar at all.
  • One account per number, rate-limited OTP, device fingerprinting for burst detection.
  • Optional enhanced verification for a higher-trust tier (raised rate limits, eligibility for verification quorums) via user-initiated DigiLocker or offline Aadhaar QR — consent-based, always optional, never required to report.
  • We do not store Aadhaar numbers. If enhanced verification is implemented, we store a verification flag and a reference token — never the number itself. The liability of an Aadhaar database vastly outweighs the product benefit.
  • Legal review is a prerequisite, not a formality. Private-entity Aadhaar authentication is constrained by the 2018 Supreme Court judgment and subsequent framework, and the DPDP Act governs anything we retain. This must be signed off by counsel before a line of it is built.

  • Officer accounts are created by invitation from an authenticated department admin, who sets department, jurisdiction and designation. There is no self-service officer signup, and no route by which a citizen account becomes an officer account.
  • The authority that grants can revoke. This is what makes transfers, retirements and suspensions work — problems no self-attested identity scheme solves.
  • Periodic re-attestation. Department admins confirm their active roster quarterly; unconfirmed accounts go read-only rather than silently retaining power.
  • Contractors and representatives follow the same rule — vouched for by the department that engaged them, scoped to specific issues, with an expiry date on the engagement.

If that badge can be obtained by anyone who passes an identity check, it is worthless. It must be obtainable only from the institution it represents.

Trust, safety & moderation

A public civic feed is a target for spam, harassment, political brigading and coordinated false reporting. These are product requirements, not policy afterthoughts.

  • Verified phone required to post. One account per number; rate-limited OTP.
  • Rate limits: 10 reports / user / day, 5 comments / issue / hour, 1 support per issue.
  • Location plausibility. Reports far from the user's GPS trail are down-weighted in the crowd count until confirmed.
  • No naming individuals. Guidelines prohibit reports targeting a private person; flagged content is hidden pending review.
  • No political content. The classifier detects and routes off-topic submissions to rejection with an explanation.
  • Officers are shielded. Officer identity on the public page is a designation and a name, never a personal contact.
  • Flag → auto-hide at 3 flags → human review. Reviewed within 24 h; the flagger and the author both receive the outcome.
  • Appeals exist. One appeal per rejection or removal, reviewed by a different person.
  • Coordinated inauthentic reporting — detected via burst patterns from similar devices or accounts; the crowd count is frozen pending review, and the issue is not deleted.
  • Transparency: the number of removed items per period is published on the public statistics page.
The gaming risk to design against If report count drives priority, report count will be gamed. Mitigation: log-scale the count, require distinct verified accounts, weight by location plausibility, and cap any single issue's crowd contribution to the priority score.

Open questions & risks

Open questions

  • Is the product called Swaram or Sanchari? The ideas document says Sanchari throughout; this document and the repository say Swaram. Trivial to fix, expensive to leave ambiguous once schemas, domains and copy are written. Needs a decision now.
  • National ambition vs. one-ward reality. The ideas document is national in scope; §12 shows we have national jurisdiction data but ~129 authority contacts. Proposal: national coverage for reporting, clustering and pressure from day one, but honest tiering on resolution, and deliberately deep onboarding in one city so at least one place demonstrates the full loop.
  • Should Swaram accept personal-safety complaints at all? The harassment case is the sharpest version of this. A confidential class makes it possible, but a civic-infrastructure product with a 3-day SLA is a bad home for an urgent personal-safety report. Proposal: accept it, route it to the police department as CONFIDENTIAL, and show the 112 interstitial first — but decide this deliberately before launch, not by default.
  • Who owns an issue that spans two departments? Proposal: a single owning department plus supporting departments as participants, never dual ownership.
  • Do representatives (corporator, MLA, minister) get accounts at launch? Their presence adds legitimacy and pressure, but also politicises the thread. Proposal: comment-only, clearly badged, and not in the MVP.
  • Do we show officer names publicly? Accountability argues yes; officer safety argues designation-only. Proposal: name + designation, no contact details, configurable per city.
  • What is the candidate radius per category? Not the merge decision — just how wide the agent casts its net before comparing images and descriptions. Generous is safe here, since location no longer decides anything on its own.
  • Should citizens see "3 similar nearby" before submitting? It may help them self-identify a duplicate, or it may discourage reporting. Proposal: show it passively, never as a prompt or a question.
  • Can citizens see internal comments after closure? Proposal: no, but the activity timeline is always public.
  • Is the feed city-wide or ward-scoped by default? Proposal: distance-based with a ward toggle.

Risks

  • Promising resolution we cannot deliver — with ~129 authority contacts nationally, most reports have no one to act on them. If the UI implies otherwise, we burn citizen trust at national scale. §12's tiering exists specifically to prevent this, and it is the single most important thing to get right in the first build.
  • The newspaper as a legal surface — auto-generated content naming departments and districts invites defamation and RTI exposure. Human review gate before the first edition, no exceptions.
  • Government non-adoption — the highest risk by far. A public feed with no officers responding is worse than nothing. Mitigation: launch in one ward with a committed department, and never make the public feed live before officers are onboarded.
  • False merges eroding trust — see §12. Officer split action is not optional.
  • The empty-feed cold start — a feed with 3 issues looks dead. Mitigation: seed with historical municipal data where available; launch geographically narrow so density is high.
  • Public visibility as a disincentive — officers may reject or under-report to protect metrics. Mitigation: rejections require a reason and are public; rejection rate is itself a tracked metric.
  • Media storage cost — 27 photos per issue at scale. Mitigation: aggressive compression, lifecycle policies, thumbnail-first delivery.
